# Observe learning and usability without overclaiming

**Scope:** Real people using a game, distinct from automated simulation-experiment and browser-only game-release-validation. Do not turn a proposed pilot into a completed study or infer a teaching effect from an agent's gameplay.

## 1. Establish the purpose and permissions

- State whether this is informal teacher feedback, formative usability testing, classroom assessment or systematic research. Requirements for school/instructor approval, consent, parental permission/assent and ethics review depend on the setting and local rules; verify before involving minors or collecting identifiable data.
- Identify who may observe, access notes or recordings, and how long data will be retained. Default to collecting the minimum data necessary. **Never publish student names, identifiers, exported audits, raw recordings or linked behavior logs in a public repository.**
- If no permission for behavioral data collection exists, use fictional examples, developer-only testing or public voluntary adult feedback as appropriate; do not quietly collect classroom analytics.

## 2. Predefine observable questions

- Prioritize 2–4 questions such as: Can a newcomer explain a decision and consequence? Can they find controls without being told? Can they distinguish a game model from a historical source? Can they explain an unexpected outcome or counterfactual?
- Define a task sequence with expected observable evidence and a neutral facilitator script. Allow players to make mistakes; ask what they think will happen before revealing an explanation.
- Sample across relevant experience and access needs when feasible, but never claim a small convenience group represents an entire school population. Use fictional student details for public demonstrations.

## 3. Observe behavior, not a retrospective story alone

- Record task attempts, choices, hesitation, unprompted comments, misinterpretations, recovery, help requested and failure mode. Separate **observed**, **participant-reported**, and **researcher-inferred** fields.
- Test a meaningful loop: entering the game, interpreting a choice, executing it, noticing its outcome, and explaining it. Include a confusing, surprising or adverse event.
- Check keyboard-only use, small screens, long passages, specialized vocabulary and culturally unfamiliar representations. Distinguish a gameplay misunderstanding from a historical misconception.
- Do not add scripted help during an observation unless the protocol records the intervention. Record facilitator prompts that may have shaped results.

## 4. Interpret and decide

- Summarize counts with explicit denominators; keep quotations brief and de-identified. List counterexamples, competing explanations and the most consequential obstacles.
- Do not infer educational effectiveness from engagement, game profit, game score or participant satisfaction. A claim of learning transfer requires an appropriately designed assessment and a defensible comparison, not just a post-game questionnaire.
- Link each proposed change to an observation and an acceptance test for the next build. Distinguish usability fixes from changes to historically consequential mechanics; the latter require game-change-review and appropriate authorization.

## Output

Produce a compact protocol before data collection, followed by a de-identified findings summary containing permissions/setting, sample and limitations, observable tasks, assistance/interventions, findings with denominators, changes proposed and unresolved questions. Keep any sensitive source material in approved restricted storage outside the public repository.
