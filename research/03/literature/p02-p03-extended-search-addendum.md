# P02/P03 — extended search and revised priorities

22 November 2026. [Research index](README.md) · [Current document requests](document-requests.md).

This exploratory addendum follows the [initial review](p02-p03-networks-and-economics-initial-review.md). It adds targeted source reading and changes an access status; it does not turn the earlier seven-record sample into a representative corpus or supply calibrated game parameters. No new model experiments were performed.

## What further searching changed

The first pass was sufficient to motivate institutional and accounting questions, but too concentrated on a few western Indian Ocean correspondents to settle them. This search sought observations that could change the mechanism under study: market demand and liquidity, material-specific recovery from loss, legal action within existing relationships, political protection costs, and a Southeast Asian comparison. It also looked for an independent documentary corpus.

### 1. Waiting can involve demand and the timing of payment obligations

PGP **5448**, approximately 1135, contains a letter from Maḍmūn in Aden to Ibn Yiju. In the English translation of T-S 20.137 recto 23–28, the sender reports weak demand until near sailing time. He says immediate collection of the tax charge would have forced pepper owners to sell below 20; he deferred collection, buyers arrived, and pepper was priced at 23 dinars per bahār. The lower price is his counterfactual claim, not an observed comparison sale. The document also describes military conflict. [E1]

**Research implication:** distinguish waiting for wind from waiting for buyers, goods, payment, or political conditions. Record who temporarily carries an obligation and who gains or loses access to spendable cash. This case cannot identify the causal return to waiting or establish a universal seasonal price curve.

The catalogue links multiple copies of this letter, including **5233, 5455, and 5479**, to the original witness. They are one document family, not repeated market observations. PGP 5233 was inspected as a catalogue/copy lead, not as a separately translated event. [E1, E2]

### 2. A voyage loss is not necessarily a uniform cargo write-off

PGP **5538**, T-S 24.64, a letter from Khalaf in Aden to Ibn Yiju on the Malabar coast, reports a larger ship wrecking at Bāb al-Mandab. Pepper was lost, while roughly half the iron was recovered by divers; salvage and transport expenses were to be deducted before proportional distribution. Relevant Goitein/Friedman English translation: recto 24–37. Its dating is broad, after 1138/39. [E3]

**Research implication:** separate damage, recoverability, recovery expenses, ownership shares, and settlement timing. This is one reported casualty. It estimates neither a general salvage probability nor the relative safety of large and small vessels.

### 3. Personal ties and legal remedies can occur in the same dispute

The letter **PGP 578**, Bodl. MS heb. d 66/5, describes prior friendship and an unsuccessful effort to recover proceeds. Its English verso 3–6 asks for an attempted settlement before court, with a judgment as the fallback. The associated mandate **PGP 534**, Bodl. MS heb. c 28/11, is dated 2 March 1085 and formally appoints representation. Both translations are credited to Mark R. Cohen. These are linked witnesses to a Mediterranean dispute, not two independent enforcement cases or an Indian Ocean observation. [E4, E5]

**Research implication:** code a sequence of demands, informal intervention, representation, and legal action. The presence of a relationship does not establish that it secured payment; the presence of a mandate does not establish recovery or a judgment.

There is also a dating trap. PGP 534's description suggests the earlier request preceded the mandate by three years, while Cohen's article p. 226 n. 32 argues for contemporaneous dating in March 1085 against Gil's earlier dating. Preserve the disagreement; do not extract a three-year collection delay from the catalogue description alone. The catalogue's cross-reference also differs in its d65/d66 shelfmark wording; the pairing here follows Cohen's explicitly identified documents. [E5, E6]

### 4. Protection, taxation, and political power may be connected

Sebastian Prange's *The Contested Sea* examines protection and conflict rather than treating maritime violence as detached from institutions. In the selected Aden discussion, pp. 21–24, he traces a shift in protection costs from private freight arrangements to state levies. His secondary account reports an additional one-dinar galley tax beside eight dinars of customs per bahār of pepper in the 1220s. The original edition cited for that example has not been read here. The ratio 1/(8+1) concerns the listed tax burden, not the commodity's sale value or an eleven-percent tariff. [E7]

**Research implication:** a levy and a protection service may need separate variables. Payment does not itself prove effective protection. Keep dynasty, route, commodity, units, and year attached to any extracted charge, and verify the original passage before calibration. The same term or charge may persist while the service changes.

### 5. Melaka offers a different institutional setting, now accessible

Hall's author upload, pp. 466–469, describes shahbandar services: lodging, storage, sales, and transport. He distinguishes Ma Huan's account from later Portuguese reports. [E8]

**Research implication:** tie services to dated offices. This later comparison cannot calibrate earlier ports. DR04 is unblocked for selected reading; underlying sources remain unchecked.

### 6. Contract categories require more than a keyword

Ackerman-Lieberman's 2011 article, now accessed through an author upload, questions an automatic equation of muʿāmala with informal, nonlegal cooperation. Its selected discussion of agency, partnership, discretion, and legal acts stresses a limited documentary cross-section. The article proposes interpretations of terminology; the labels should not be treated as universal or uncontested across schools, communities, and periods. Relevant printed pp. 653–661. [E9]

**Research implication:** retain the original term, the actual obligations described, the interpreter's classification, and the confidence level. Co-occurrence of a partnership word and a person's name is not a sufficient network edge or financing model.

## The largest remaining gap: independent observations

Additional letters from the same correspondents can improve a case but do not remove selection bias. A genuinely useful next comparison is the **Quseir Arabic documentary corpus**, represented by Li Guo's editions of business letters and shipping notes/accounts. The editions were located; their bodies have not been accessed. No claim about their numerical contents or equivalence to the Geniza sample is made here. First check their date, genre, transaction scale, preservation, and editorial revisions before comparing anything. [E10, E11]

An additional regional lead is **De Simone and Trinco (2025)** on Kerala's Jain merchants. The authors' institutional abstract identifies Kannada/Malayalam inscriptions and material evidence linking merchant associations and inland/coastal routes. At the 22 November checkpoint the article body was unavailable. A user-supplied copy resolved that access gap on 23 November; selected body passages and findings are recorded in the dated follow-up below. This could widen the evidence base beyond overseas correspondents, but the abstract does not establish a complete trade network or any price/cost estimate. [E12]

No new confirmatory sweep should precede a protocol. Existing R0 results, the initial review, and these source discoveries are prior looks. Define the question before choosing which episodes to quantify. Include failures, unresolved accounts, and contrary cases; do not select cases solely because they support a planned mechanic.

## 23 November follow-up: chronology, account, vessel and Kerala

These are bounded source findings, not adopted game rules or calibrated parameters. The chronology audit inspected [KVIS main at f4757265](https://github.com/Azuromalachite/kvis-econ-games-2026/blob/f47572651fe6ef09e3ac7ff1329536cbc7a542f7/projects/03/index.html) and its recorded upstream Atlas source `3394aa2`; it describes that snapshot, not every later revision.

### C01 — Chronology and institutional setting

Atlas already discloses c. 1000–1450 as a composite and acknowledges Song–Yuan Quanzhou alongside fifteenth-century Melaka. An early fifteenth-century research anchor would bring Melaka, Ming goods and the imperial-fleet context closer together, but it is a proposal rather than an owner-adopted scenario.

Ma Huan/Mills's Melaka passages, pp. 108–114, describe fleet storage and seasonal assembly; Calicut, pp. 137–145, includes brokered agreements; Aden, pp. 154–158, describes an expedition and gives the Rasulid context (1229–1454). These particular arrangements, including imperial transactions, do not establish universal private-merchant access. [E13]

UNESCO's Quanzhou designation concerns tenth–fourteenth-century Song–Yuan prosperity; that scope does not demonstrate closure in 1368. Fleisher et al. describe fourteenth–fifteenth-century maritime architecture at Kilwa, drawing on earlier excavations. The original excavation reports were not checked. Together these sources support period-aware comparison, not one merchant's entire five-port circuit. [E14, E15]

**Permitted wording:** the game is a disclosed teaching composite. Generic D money, fixed tariffs, price curves, capacities and hazard effects remain model assumptions. An imperial treasure fleet is not interchangeable with a private merchant's purchasable ship.

This extends the copper subtotal in the initial review rather than adding another independent transaction. The reported closing total is 535⅓, compared with incoming credits of 535. That difference remains unresolved; it is not silently corrected or classified as a manuscript error.

Exact rational arithmetic checked 315, 220, 425⅛ and the ⅓ difference. Dispatch does not establish arrival or resale. Original acquisition costs are absent, so this is not voyage profit. Bahars are not converted to kilograms and dinars are not equated to game D. The twelfth-century account is an earlier analogue for a proposed fifteenth-century scenario.

### C03 — Bakau/Maranei vessel–cargo check

Flecker's early fifteenth-century wreck study reports surviving hull measurements of 22.7 m by 6.5 m at the widest measurable point and Chinese construction evidence, with Thai, Vietnamese and Chinese ceramics and storage jars. The remains are not a complete vessel envelope or intact manifest: substantial recovery preceded the short survey. Contents of jars are not identified sufficiently to call them pepper. Radiocarbon, Yongle coins and ceramics support broad dating, not an exact sinking date. Relevant pp. 222–229 were read through the author-uploaded text; clear figures were not independently checked. [E16]

Miksic places the vessel in the context of Ming maritime restrictions and interprets the voyage as smuggling. This is an attributed historical interpretation, not a recovered operating licence or proof that every Chinese commercial voyage was illicit. Selected pp. 1640–1642 read. [E17]

**Permitted inference:** a Chinese trading vessel with mixed ceramics is a useful qualitative comparator. This does not establish 300 tonnes of capacity, the game's 2.5× capacity ratio, porcelain lot weights or a storm-damage advantage. “Chinese trading junk” is a proposed label for review, not a game change made here.

### C04 — Recovered Kerala study: explicit evidence versus interpretation

De Simone and Trinco's article is now received (DR11). Its 1204 Belagavi inscription excerpts, pp. 346–349, record an assembly's temple revenue through commodity-specific contributions in goods or money. These are not a universal port customs schedule or completed sales. The inscription's explicitly Jain merchant groups and its separately named Kerala merchants must not be collapsed: the latter's religion is not explicitly given (p. 349). [E12]

Pages 351–352 distinguish Malayalam evidence for Jain-associated merchant institutions from the absence of direct epigraphic identification of Jain Kerala horse traders. The militia/protection discussion concerns institutions and their assets; it supplies no per-passage ship-guard wage, contract duration or turn-back rule. Footnote 18's suggested military origins and profit shares of mummuridaṇḍas are attributed interpretations, not a documented standard hiring contract.

Pages 363–366 connect inland routes, waterways, forest products and Jain remains, while explicitly qualifying evidence for gemstone specialisation. **Permitted inference:** investigate port–hinterland procurement and institution-specific obligations. Do not assign automatic religion-based bonuses, complete trade-network edges, numerical supply rates or fees from this article. Selected relevant passages read; original Kannada/Malayalam editions and all cited archaeological publications were not independently checked.
