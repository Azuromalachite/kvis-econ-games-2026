## Findings

### Chapter 9, "Commodities and markets"

- **Competing arrivals spoil markets.** The chance arrival of another ship carrying the same kind of cargo could ruin the market for ships already in port, so local merchants tried to keep track of shipping movements (183).
- **Jedda, 1724.** After harvest failures in Yemen, fourteen grain ships from Egypt reached Jedda, five more were expected and five were reported lost; Malabar rice exporters to Mocha found provisions selling cheaply (183–184, note 3, unread). Chaudhuri concludes trade was not pure chance but fluctuated strongly from year to year (184).
- **Bulk cargo and departures.** Merchants feared a shortage of saleable bulk goods to balance fine cargo; dates served as ballast and as the measure of shipping space (184). Indian ships at the Shatt al-Arab, already loaded with gold and silver coin, could be kept waiting for the date boats from Baghdad until the monsoon made further delay dangerous, then sailed with non-commercial ballast, at a financial and reputational cost (184). No date or source is given for this example.
- **Map 18** (pp. 186–187): "Main regional exports from the Indian Ocean before 1750". Each region has a circle divided into three classes (bulk and low-value goods; luxury and high-value goods; gold and silver) with a list of commodities. Circles differ in size, but there is no key for size or segment share, no units and no stated source, so **no quantity can be read from it**. It is not reproduced here.
- **Basra, 1729.** In Cowan's correspondence, the unexpected arrival of the *Fame* with goods not meant for Basra harmed the sale of the *William*'s cargo, which had been sent there; the *William*'s voyage lost twenty per cent (195). No note marker follows the Basra sentences; notes 17–19 later in the same paragraph attach to other examples, and whether any of them cites this letter is unknown until the endnotes are read. Unverified.
- **Anticipating gluts.** Cowan tried to anticipate the market to avoid over-supply: in 1729 he sent Bengal sugar to Cambay to be sold quickly, expecting many sugar ships (195, note 19, unread).
- **What a great port provided** (197). Chaudhuri lists four services a wholesale dealer needed at Aden, Calicut, Malacca or Canton: "spot" or forward markets with clear price indicators and continuity of supply; bankers' credit and the exchange of local sale proceeds for foreign bills; settlement of accounts and recovery of debts, by merchant consensus or the courts; and a freight market for shipping space. Merchants with high-value goods spread them over many ships. He calls this a schema reached by deduction, corroborated by evidence after the seventeenth century, and says how far back it holds is uncertain (197).
- **Storing until scarce.** Al-Idrisi, quoted on Daybul (Debal) in Sind, says its inhabitants bought in bulk and stored goods until the ships had left and the goods became scarce (197–198, note 21, unread). Chaudhuri reads this as storage in expectation of better prices (198).
- **Spot or forward** (198). A travelling buyer could deal with stock-holding spot dealers, or leave an order with a broker for the coming season, which Chaudhuri calls a form of forward dealing. Mocha and Beit al-Fakih coffee was sold from dealers' warehouses stocked before the ships arrived. Spices, coffee, tea and food grains changed hands mostly in spot markets.
- **Yemeni coffee** (199, notes 22–23, unread). The main factors were the size of the annual crop and the number of ships actually visiting, complicated by anticipation; news of prices at Jedda and Cairo moved Mocha prices at once. Dealers kept marking prices up after the season opened; once buyers no longer expected more ships, prices drifted down. By one English buyer's account from the 1720s, bulk buyers got no customary price reduction at Mocha or Beit al-Fakih, while buying in small quantities posed no problem. Large dealers could move prices by releasing or withholding stock, and peasant growers skipped the market when they judged the journey unprofitable.
- **Rulers and pepper** (200). Indonesian ports supplied pepper from spot markets at supply-and-demand prices, but from time to time strong rulers in Malabar and South East Asia cornered supplies and sold at semi-monopoly prices. Canton tea through the Hongs is given as a parallel.
- **Advances and forward goods** (200–202). Indigo and cotton piece-goods are Chaudhuri's two classic forward-market goods (200). At Biana, wealthy dealers advanced money to indigo growers before the harvest (200, after Pelsaert; his account and its edition are cited only in the unread endnotes, so unverified). For export textiles, advance contracts assured merchants of supply before the sailing dates and protected weavers from unsold stock (201); a part payment bound the weaver to deliver, and the price was either fixed in advance or set at delivery (202); agents sought weavers from October to December (202). Chaudhuri rejects reading this as the European "putting-out" system: the merchant stayed mainly a trader and financed production secondarily, and independent weavers also sold locally (201). Highly specialised cloth could not be sold locally if the contracting merchant failed to take delivery (198).
- **Three kinds of market and trader** (202): great merchant families, specialised merchants, and bazaars and pedlars, the last probably marginal to seaborne volume.

### Chapter 10, "Capital and trade in the Indian Ocean"

- **Time between outlay and return** (203). Long-distance trade separates the initial investment, the despatch and sale in distant markets, and the final returns, and that interval is what makes it a business of capital. Payments separated by time and space recur at 214–215.
- **Small, divided transactions** (204–205). Individual merchants and partnerships handled small quantities relative to the economy (204). A letter written in Alexandria, c. 1058–9 (Geniza period; source in note 1, unread), shows consignments split into small lots carried by different people. Chaudhuri says professional merchants divided their risks and operations, and had reasons not to make centralisation their only method of trade (205). Elsewhere goods are consigned from agent to agent (189–190) and spread over many ships (197).
- **Instruments of credit** (210). Contract and partnership law existed, including commenda contracts by which a junior merchant borrowed capital from a senior one. Bonds raised on a ship or its cargo carried a fixed interest, repaid at the overseas port or at the end of the voyage, with the lender bearing the risk of shipwreck. Bills of exchange drawn between port towns were the most flexible instrument. No note marker in this paragraph. Little is known about how credit was first established (210).
- **Banking instruments versus metal** (217). Bills of exchange, personal bonds and discounted bills were known, but their weight against gold and silver in financing trade cannot be established.
- **Known markets, uncertain balance** (219). Merchants knew which goods a market wanted and roughly how much it would absorb; speculation and temporary imbalances still created uncertainty. Merchants advanced money to producers against delivery on time and to quality (219–220, note 18, unread). The remark on roughly how much a market would absorb (219) is the nearest thing in these chapters to the registered depth cap; it gives no quantity and cannot inform that protocol's ranges.

## What remains unresolved

- **Endnotes.** Neither file contains them. Every primary attribution above (Jedda 1724, Cowan, al-Idrisi, the coffee reports, Pelsaert, the letter of c. 1058–9) rests on Chaudhuri's body text. DR01 and DR02 asked for the notes; they remain outstanding.
- **Primary sources.** None was checked. The Shatt al-Arab delay (184) and the credit instruments on p. 210 carry no note marker at all; no marker follows the Basra 1729 sentences (195), though notes 17–19 appear later in that paragraph for other examples.
- **Edition.** The files have no title or copyright page; the edition rests on the Crossref record.
- **No numerical calibration.** The chapters give no price elasticities, absorption capacities, settlement delays, interest rates or discounts usable as game values. The one rate-like figure, the *William*'s 20% loss (195), is a single reported outcome. Map 18 is not quantitative.
- **Period.** The detailed evidence is mostly seventeenth- and eighteenth-century and drawn from European sources for want of Asian business records (196); Chaudhuri extends it to earlier centuries by inference (197).

## Bearing on game mechanisms

The game's price model, read from `projects/03/index.html` at `main` `cedef87` (file SHA-256 `5c722df9d8432d9a5df763d8a5a460b6ccbb9f4e1730ae008a8e58908ff8dbd3`):

- `getLivePortPrice` (`:2111-2117`) returns max(10, round(base × (1 + 0.08 × sin(π(month + s)/6)))), where base is that good's price at that port and *s* is the character code of the port id's first letter plus that of the good id's first letter.
- So the "universal sin(±8%) wave" is close but inexact. Each (port, good) pair has its own base price. Every good shares the same form: amplitude ±8%, a 12-month period, rounding and a 10 D floor. Phase depends only on (first letter of port + first letter of good) mod 12. Teak/textiles and porcelain/pepper share a phase at every port, and Aden and Malacca share every phase (character codes 97 and 109 differ by 12).
- Prices depend on nothing else: not on the quantity traded, the player's earlier sales, other ships, news or harvests. Buying pays the live price plus tariff (`:2190-2191`); selling credits the live price to cash at once (`:2216-2220`); month-24 liquidation uses month min(23, monthIndex) (`:2610`).

Against that, the chapters describe prices driven by arrivals, crops and news (183–184, 195, 199), different market forms for different goods (198–202), and gaps in time between outlay and return (203, 210). They give direction and vocabulary, not magnitudes.

## Ideas, not registered

These are ideas only. None is registered, none has a protocol or prediction, and none may be added to the registered depth-and-liquidity protocol. Each would need its own versioned experiment, and any game change goes upstream as `projects/03/README.md` requires.

- **Sale, cash-receipt and payment timing.** Sources: 203, 210, 214–215. Today a sale is cash at once. The registered protocol already varies a hypothetical settlement delay; these chapters were read after it was frozen and cannot be used to choose or adjust its values. A different form (payment at a later port, a bond repaid at voyage end, a bill drawn on another port) would be a new experiment.
- **Supply and competing arrivals.** Sources: 183–184, 195, 199. Other ships' cargoes moving a port's price. The registered protocol depresses prices only through the player's own sales; arrivals by others are a separate mechanism.
- **Forward contracts.** Sources: 198, 200–202, 219–220. An order placed for a later season, with a part payment, delivery before a sailing date, price fixed in advance or at delivery, and a loss if the buyer fails to collect. Not the passage bookings in the specialist-contracts protocol.
- **Per-good price functions.** Sources: 198–200. Spot goods priced by crop and arrivals (coffee, spices, grain), forward goods priced by contract (textiles, indigo), pepper sometimes cornered. This would replace the shared ±8% form above, not merely re-phase it. Pamuk (2004) adds only a methodological caution ([reading note](2026-11-24-pamuk-2004-reading-note.md)).
