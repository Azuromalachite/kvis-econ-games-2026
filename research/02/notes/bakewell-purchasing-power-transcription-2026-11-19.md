## Food purchasing power

**Bakewell reports alternative-food purchasing power for a five-day wage spread over seven consumption days.**

Source: pp. 102–104, especially the table on p. 104 and footnotes 52–53 on pp. 102–103. The table is a reconstruction from sparse early-seventeenth-century prices and declared measurement assumptions, not observed dietary intake.

| Occupation | Cash wage, reales per five-day week | Potato kcal/day | Maize kcal/day | Charqui kcal/day |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Mine worker | 20 | 5,200 | 2,800 | 3,300 |
| Ore freighter | 17.5 | 4,500 | 2,400 | 2,900 |
| Refinery worker | 15 | 3,900 | 2,100 | 2,500 |

Every food column spends the entire wage on that food alone. They cannot be summed into a basket. Figures are rounded to the nearest 100 kcal. Oca and chuño appear in the mass table on p. 103, but no calorie estimates for them are supplied in this table.

The [machine-readable transcription](../evidence/bakewell-purchasing-power-1603.json) preserves all three wage rows, the mass table, units, price context and the source's conversion assumptions. It deliberately contains no experiment-2b outcome.

## Who receives which wage

**Bakewell distinguishes extra hired workers from substitutes and identifies a separate payment by the substitute's engager.**

Pages 122–125 describe different arrangements. The roughly 1600 rate of 56–72 reales per five-day week concerns skilled hired ore cutters; substitute ore cutters receive that amount from those engaging them and also collect 20 reales in regular mita wages. Ore compensation is additional, variable and not priced in this transcription. A substitute replaces a drafted worker rather than adding another worker to the mine.

The earlier Capoche passages and the turn-of-century rates remain separate. Skill, recruitment and employment access are not assumed identical for all households.

## Currency clarification

**The wage passages explicitly pair eight reales with one ordinary peso.**

Pages 124–125 give paired ordinary-peso/reales amounts. This resolves the earlier specification's statement that this conversion was only inferred from the Godoy arithmetic. It does not collapse pesos corrientes and pesos ensayados into one currency: footnote 52–53's price conversions remain separately recorded.

The 1608 Godoy costs on p. 105 are a distinct retail-cost estimate for one mitayo. They are not re-estimated from the 1603 purchasing-power table, and the third place in the printed compound money notation is not assigned an unverified denomination here.
