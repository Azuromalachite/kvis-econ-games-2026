## Finding
With food priced from Bakewell's 1603 reconstruction instead of the judgement range, compulsory mita service and the
mixed case still lowered the household's food in every draw, for every food. Hired ore cutting raised it in 12.7% of
draws when the cash bought potatoes, 7.0% for maize and 7.9% for charqui. **The prediction that the verdict band would
not change missed for potatoes**: 12.7% moves hired work from "under 10%" into the conditional "10% to under 50%" band.
Under experiment 2's decision rule, the game keeps its rule that wages do not buy food, labelled as a simplification,
with this result and its conditions as the basis.

## Checks before any comparison
- The experiment-2 draw array was regenerated with its own routine and seed (all six columns) and reproduced experiment
  2's published shares exactly: mita 0, hired 0.034, mixed 0.
- Only then was each draw's `kcal_per_real` replaced with one fixed documented value. The original array was checked
  unchanged after the run.
- Calendar units as declared: five paid days, seven consumption days; the printed daily calories are multiplied by 7
  and divided by the weekly wage, never by 5 again. Foods are never summed; oca and chuño (no printed calories) are out.

## Why cheaper food helps hired work only a little and hurts mita
In this model a larger kcal-per-real figure multiplies the season's net cash, whatever its sign. Mita wages (20 reales a
week) never cover the Godoy costs (46–77 a week), so a cheaper food makes the deficit cost more food: mita gets worse.
Hired wages (56–72) cover costs only when the costs are low and many weeks are paid; in those draws cheaper food
multiplies a surplus. Potatoes are cheapest per calorie, so they tip the most draws.

## Limits stated in advance, and as they bear here
- The costs are still Godoy's 1608 retail estimate for a mitayo, held fixed while prices come from a 1603
  reconstruction: a conditional comparison, not a coherent reconstruction of one year.
- The printed values are Bakewell's reconstruction from sparse prices and stated conversions, rounded to 100 kcal; no
  uncertainty is fitted around them.
- Spending a whole wage on one food is the source's device for comparing purchasing power, not a diet.
- Substitute premiums, ore income and family earnings are left out, as registered; mitayos often brought their
  families (Gil Montero 2011), which the one-worker budget does not represent. Any of these is a separate experiment.
- A model result, not a finding about what households in the 1500s ate.
