    GDP grew. Did life get better?

    Angus Maddison's estimates, informed guesses for all of Latin America, have output per person rising a little across the 1500s, even as the population collapsed. GDP counts everything produced, including silver shipped across the world. It does not show who ate. Through six harvests you will watch two numbers side by side: GDP per person and food per person.

    You run an ayllu of about 1,000 people

    Your fields climb the mountain from warm maize valleys below about 3,500 m to the frozen puna above 4,000 m. Higher fields freeze more often. In this game frost always comes down from the top, so the valley is your safest field, and you send crews uphill only because the lower zones are full. Each year you send about 14 work crews (fewer if people die) to plant, build terraces, or freeze-dry potatoes into chuño for your community stores.

    You play six harvests, each one farming year. Harvests 1–3 are three years in a row under the Inca, around 1500. Harvests 4–6 are sample years from c. 1575, c. 1592 and c. 1600, after the Spanish colonial mita began sending one man in seven from the communities of the mita region (yours is one) to the silver mines at Potosí. Between them, decades pass. Your goal is to feed everyone 1,700 kcal a day.

    Why farm the cold heights?

    In this game, spreading crews uphill adds room, not insurance: the game uses a simplified frost boundary, so its frost always strikes the highest fields first. Terrain and exposure also affect real frost risk.

    Each height grows something the others can't. The vertical archipelago interpretation (the anthropologist John Murra's term) describes access to resources at different heights, often in separate places. Whether that buffers a bad year depends on which places and crops are affected.

    In the game, each zone has room for only 5 crews (more once you build terraces or raised fields). So feeding everyone means sending some crews uphill, where frost is more likely. The game leaves out bad years that hit some heights and spare others, which is why spreading never pays here. (One physical inference, not a documented historical pattern: cold air draining downhill can freeze a lower field while a higher one is spared.)

        How the model works: which numbers carry the lesson, and which are only for play

        Carry the lesson (change these and the result can change)

        • Colonial quotas set at the 1573 count. The mita takes one man in seven of the 1573 population and tribute is a set total. Tribute could be reassessed after a new count; mita allocations followed a different adjustment process. The game keeps both fixed, a simplification, so the burden per person rises as people die. This follows Toledo's system; the size of the tribute (500 kcal per person per day at the 1573 count, taken in food) is a game figure.
        • Inca labour duty: one man in ten on state and temple fields, half of that harvest returned as feasts and rations. Teaching assumption; how much came back is debated. The game also leaves out exchange: Andean communities traded and used markets as well as relying on redistribution (Hirth & Pillsbury 2013), so the Inca years here are simpler than the real economy.
        • Land per person: output in a zone grows with labour to the power 0.9, so survivors of an epidemic each farm a little more land. The exponent is close to 1 because terraces need labour to keep up (Wernke 2010 describes terraces abandoned as people were lost). Teaching assumption.
        • Resettlement: Toledo's reducciones moved families into nucleated towns, often far from their fields (Wernke 2010). The change came gradually as distant fields and terraces were given up, and it varied from place to place, with some communities negotiating to keep their land (Hunter 2025; de la Puente Luna & Perales 2026). The game lowers colonial yields by 7% at c. 1575, 12% at c. 1592 and 15% at c. 1600; the sizes are teaching assumptions.
        • Sampled years: harvests 1–3 are consecutive years, but harvests 4–6 are snapshots of c. 1575, 1592 and 1600. Stores, buildings and people carry over between snapshots as a teaching simplification; the game does not model the decades in between (their harvests, losses, births or migration).
        • Population: a 50% fall before 1573 and 15% more in the 1585–91 epidemics. Historians' estimates vary widely; these are round game figures.
        • Stores across the 70 years: the game jumps from harvest 3 (c. 1502) to harvest 4 (c. 1575) and keeps each person's share of the community stores across the gap, so chuño stored under the Inca still feeds people after 1573. Carrying the same food across this roughly seventy-year gap is a teaching simplification; intervening consumption and storage losses are not modeled. Emptying them at 1573 would cut colonial food by about 120 kcal a day with the default plan (measured 2026-11-18); the lesson holds either way.
        • Re-planning when crews fall: whenever crews fall (at 1573, after the 1585–91 epidemics, and after hunger deaths) you must re-plan fewer crews: keep your plan's shares, put every crew on the best land first, or set them yourself. The choice is yours, and it decides much of the result. With the default plan and seeds 1–80, best land first gives colonial food of about 1,385 kcal a day and keeping the same shares about 1,000; with fewer chuño crews in the plan, keeping the shares can grow more. The re-plan box shows both for the plan you hold. Farming only the best land leaves no chuño crews and nothing growing up high. Before 18 November 2026 the board made this choice silently, always best land first.
        • Zone yields: the valley yields most and the puna least. With the re-plan above, this sets how much output per person rises after the collapse.
        • Silver versus food in GDP: GDP per person = 340 + 0.045 × field output + silver. In this game's formula chuño crews add nothing to GDP (preserving food is not counted as output here), so moving crews from chuño to the fields raises measured GDP even though nobody eats more. A mine worker's silver counts somewhat more than a farm worker's output. Teaching assumptions, tuned so the Inca years start near Maddison's 416; the "reverse" panel shows what happens if they are different.

        Only for play (they change single years, not the lesson)

        • Frost odds and frost losses, drought odds and size, the Pleiades forecast's accuracy (the forecasting practice itself is real: Orlove, Chiang & Cane 2000).
        • The winter after each harvest: hard (1 in 4), normal or mild (1 in 4), letting each chuño crew make 130%, 100% or 70% of its usual amount. Chuño really is made with night frost and daytime sun (FAO Mountain Partnership); how much the weather changes the pace is a play value. Over 80 seeds it leaves the averages unchanged but moves single runs by up to about 100 kcal a day.
        • Terrace and raised-field effects. Raised fields protect against frost through their water and shape, and how much depends on the field (Kolata & Ortloff 1989), so the game gives waru waru more frost protection than terraces; the sizes are play values. Also room per zone, chuño capacity, the starting community stores, the 1,900 kcal eating cap and spoilage.
        • Deaths from hunger, the wage crews' tribute relief, and the 30% household spread behind "households below 1,700".

        How the numbers are shown

        • GDP as an index (Maddison's 1500 level, 416, = 100). Historical GDP series come in different dollars: Maddison (2003) uses 1990 international dollars, the newer Maddison Project Database (Bolt & van Zanden) uses 2011 dollars and gives Peru-specific estimates for the early 1600s. An index lets the game compare with either without mixing units. On the chart, the diamond is Maddison's 1600 estimate for all of Latin America, 5% above 1500. Because the game was tuned to start near 1500's level, matching it there is a design choice, not a test.
        • Caloric Welfare Index, out of 100: 50 points for food adequacy, 30 for stores, 20 for farming resilience. The weights come from the original game's classroom model; how each part is scored here (food against 1,700 kcal, stores against 90 days, resilience from the crews on terraces or raised fields that stood before the harvest, counted against the community's full 14 crews and scaled by how full the stores are, so a community with empty stores scores no resilience) is this adaptation's assumption. It does not reward spreading crews across zones, because in this game spreading never pays. It is a model output, not a measurement.
        • Silver. In this game, silver output per mine worker rises in later harvests (from harvest 4 to harvest 6), a teaching assumption.

The original author built the game around one message: GDP per person grew in sixteenth-century Latin America, but life did not get better, because food per person fell. He drew on Maddison's historical statistics and his familiarity with global economic history, and also used the game to teach the quipu.

        The game is a model, not a reconstruction. Anchors: GDP per person of 416 in 1500 and 438 in 1600 are Maddison's conjectural estimates for Latin America as a whole (1990 international dollars; not Andes-specific), and the game's GDP formula was tuned to start near 416. The fall from about 1,700 to about 1,200 kcal is the original game's teaching figure and has no source we could confirm. Here “kcal per person per day” means average food available across all ages, capped at 1,900. The household share assumes household food varies 30% around the average; it is a guess that illustrates the point Wernke & Whitmore (2009, Human Ecology 37:421) make about production, that some households' own fields fall short while the community has a surplus (their model estimates what fields produced, not what people ate). Every other number is the game's own. For scale, adults in a modern multi-country sample that excluded manual labourers used about 2,190 (women) and 2,720 (men) kcal a day (Pontzer et al. 2016, Nature 533:390, Table 1); a community average that includes children is lower.
